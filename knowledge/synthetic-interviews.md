# Synthetic User Interviews — What the Fridge

## Introduction

Synthetic interviews are AI-generated user research scenarios designed to simulate the kinds of conversations a product team would have with real users during early-stage discovery. They are not a replacement for genuine user research — nothing substitutes for sitting across from a real person and hearing their unscripted frustrations. Instead, synthetic interviews serve as a complement: they help a design team anticipate edge cases, stress-test assumptions, and map the diversity of potential user needs before investing time and resources in recruiting and scheduling actual participants.

These five interviews were generated to explore a broad range of household types, cooking skill levels, budget constraints, and life circumstances that the "What the Fridge" app might encounter in real-world use. Each scenario was crafted to push on a different dimension of the problem space — from a single professional wasting produce mid-week, to a recently divorced man learning to feed himself for the first time. The goal is to surface design questions and feature priorities that might not emerge from a narrow initial user base.

The use of synthetic interviews is an accepted practice in agentic project design and lean UX methodology, particularly for early-stage exploration (BUS 860, University of Alberta — EMBA program). They provide a structured way to think through user needs before committing to a real research protocol. The interviews that follow should be treated as hypotheses about user behavior — useful for generating design ideas and prioritization frameworks, but always subject to validation through actual user testing.

## Interview 1: "The Busy Professional"

### Participant Profile

| Field | Details |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Anika Patel |
| **Age range** | 30-34 |
| **Household size** | 1 (lives alone) |
| **Cooking comfort level** | Intermediate — can follow recipes, knows basic techniques, but rarely experiments |
| **Typical weekly grocery spend** | $70-90 CAD |

### Context

Anika is a project manager at a consulting firm in Edmonton. She works 50+ hours most weeks, with unpredictable evening commitments. She does one big grocery shop on Sunday, usually at Superstore, and tries to buy "healthy stuff" but often finds wilted greens and expired yogurt by Wednesday. She knows she wastes food but feels trapped in the cycle — she buys with good intentions, then reverts to toast or takeout when she's too tired to figure out what to cook.

### Interview Transcript

**Interviewer:** Thanks for sitting down with me, Anika. Can you walk me through what a typical weeknight looks like when you get home and need to eat?

**Anika:** Sure. So, most nights I get home around 7, sometimes later if there's a client thing. I'm tired. I open the fridge and I just... stare at it. There's stuff in there — like, I can see bell peppers, some chicken thighs, half a bag of spinach — but my brain can't put it together into a meal. It's not that I can't cook. I just can't *decide* what to cook. So I end up making scrambled eggs on toast or ordering Uber Eats, and then the chicken sits there until Thursday when I throw it out.

**Interviewer:** That moment of staring into the fridge — how often would you say that happens?

**Anika:** Honestly? Four out of five weeknights. Sundays are great. I come back from the store, I'm motivated, I might even make a stir-fry or prep some rice. But by Tuesday the motivation is gone, and I'm just tired. The worst part is I *know* I have food. I just can't think of what to do with it.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned you shop on Sundays. Do you plan meals before you go?

**Anika:** Kind of? I'll think "I should eat more vegetables this week" and buy a bunch of produce. But I don't plan specific meals for specific days. I've tried that — writing out Monday: salmon, Tuesday: pasta — but my schedule changes so much that the plan falls apart by Monday night. Then I feel guilty that I didn't follow the plan and I just give up on the whole thing.

**Interviewer:** If an app could look at what's in your fridge right now and suggest what to cook tonight, what would make that suggestion actually useful to you?

**Anika:** Speed. I need it to be something I can make in 30 minutes or less. And it needs to use the stuff that's about to go bad first. Like, if my spinach is on day five, don't suggest a recipe that uses the canned beans that will last another year. Tell me to use the spinach tonight. Oh, and portion size matters. Every recipe online is for four people. I'm one person. I don't want to eat the same pasta for four days.

**Interviewer:** What about breakfast? You mentioned eggs on toast — is breakfast something you think about or just autopilot?

**Anika:** Total autopilot. It's either toast with peanut butter, or a banana, or nothing if I'm running late. Honestly, I'd love some variety, but mornings are so rushed. If someone told me "you have everything to make overnight oats, and it takes two minutes to prep tonight," I'd actually do that. But I never think of it in the moment. My brain is already on work by 6:30 AM.

**Interviewer:** When you throw food away — what does that feel like?

**Anika:** Awful. Genuinely awful. I think about the money, but also just the waste. Last week I threw out an entire container of cherry tomatoes and half a cucumber. That's like four dollars, but it's also just... I bought those because I was going to be a person who eats salad for lunch. And then I wasn't. It feels like a small failure every time.

**Interviewer:** If this app existed and worked well for you, what would success look like after a week of using it?

**Anika:** I think success would be throwing away less than one item per week. Right now it's probably four or five things. And actually eating something that isn't toast for at least three dinners. It doesn't need to be fancy. I just want to feel like I'm using what I bought and not wasting money. Oh — and if it could tell me Sunday morning, "buy these eight things and you'll have enough for the whole week," that would be incredible. Like a shopping list that's actually connected to reality.

### Key Insights

- **"Use this first" prioritization** is critical for users who shop infrequently. The app needs to surface items nearing expiration at the top of meal suggestions, not just match ingredients to recipes.
- **Single-serving recipe scaling** is a real gap in existing recipe resources. The app should default to suggesting meals for one (or the household size in the profile) rather than making users mentally divide portions.
- **Breakfast is an overlooked opportunity.** Even intermediate cooks default to autopilot in the morning. Low-effort breakfast suggestions (especially ones that can be prepped the night before) could be a differentiating feature.

## Interview 2: "The Family Juggler"

### Participant Profile

| Field | Details |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Marcus Thompson |
| **Age range** | 40-44 |
| **Household size** | 4 (spouse + two kids, ages 7 and 11) |
| **Cooking comfort level** | Confident — enjoys cooking, tries new recipes on weekends |
| **Typical weekly grocery spend** | $200-250 CAD |

### Context

Marcus and his wife Janelle both work full-time. Marcus handles most of the cooking because he genuinely enjoys it. Dinners are usually planned — he picks three or four recipes on the weekend and shops accordingly. But breakfast is a rotating disaster of cereal and toast, and lunches are an afterthought. The kids are picky: his 7-year-old refuses anything with visible onions, and his 11-year-old has decided she doesn't like "mixed-together food" (casseroles, stews, stir-fries). He consistently overbuys produce, especially when it's on sale.

### Interview Transcript

**Interviewer:** Marcus, you sound like someone who actually enjoys cooking. Where does the process break down for you?

**Marcus:** Dinners are fine. I've got a system for dinners. The problem is everything else. Breakfast is chaos — the kids want different things, my wife grabs a coffee on her way out, and I'm trying to get lunches packed while making sure the 7-year-old actually eats something before school. Lunches are the real black hole. I end up throwing together sandwiches or — honestly — sometimes I just buy those Lunchables things because I'm out of ideas and out of time.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the overbuying. What happens?

**Marcus:** Okay, classic example from last week. Superstore had a big bag of broccoli crowns on sale. Great. I bought two bags because it was a good deal. We used one bag Monday night for a stir-fry. The second bag? Still sitting in the fridge on Friday when I noticed it was turning yellow. Into the green bin it went. Same thing happens with cilantro, green onions, berries — anything that goes bad fast. I buy it with a vague plan, use some, and the rest rots.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned your kids are picky eaters. How does that affect what you cook?

**Marcus:** It's a constant negotiation. My daughter Zoe — she's 11 — she'll eat the individual components of a meal but not if they're "mixed together." So if I make a curry, I have to basically deconstruct it for her: rice here, chicken here, sauce on the side. My son Eli won't eat anything with visible onion, garlic pieces, or herbs. He'll eat onion *powder* in things — he just can't see it. So I'm basically cooking the same meal three different ways, which is exhausting and generates more dirty dishes than seems physically possible.

**Interviewer:** If this app suggested a dinner, how would it need to account for your household?

**Marcus:** It would need to know that Eli has his thing about visible onions and Zoe has her thing about mixed textures. And then it would need to suggest a base recipe that I can easily adapt for both of them without cooking three separate meals. Like, if it suggests tacos — perfect. Everyone assembles their own. Stew? Not going to work. The app should know the difference.

**Interviewer:** Let's talk about the leftover problem. Do you currently repurpose dinner leftovers into lunches?

**Marcus:** I *should.* My wife actually asks me to do this. She'll say "just pack the leftover pasta for the kids' lunches." But the kids won't eat reheated pasta from a thermos — they say it tastes different. What actually works is when I can transform leftovers into something new. Like if we have leftover roast chicken, I can shred it for wraps the next day. But I only think of that sometimes. If the app could say "you'll have leftover chicken from tonight — here's a 5-minute wrap for tomorrow's lunch," that would be amazing. That connection between dinner and tomorrow's lunch is where I lose the thread.

**Interviewer:** If your wife could see the meal plan too — would that be useful?

**Marcus:** Extremely. Right now she texts me at 3 PM asking "what's for dinner" and I either have an answer or I panic-plan something. If she could see the plan on her phone, she could even pick up a missing ingredient on her way home. And she could add stuff — like if she knows Eli has a friend coming over, she could flag that we need to plan for five people that night instead of four.

**Interviewer:** What would make you trust this app enough to actually follow its plan?

**Marcus:** It would need to prove it understands my family. If the first suggestion it gives me is a deconstructed-nightmare casserole that Zoe won't eat, I'm deleting the app. But if it says "Taco Tuesday — Eli gets his without onions, Zoe assembles her own, and you'll have leftover seasoned beef for Wednesday's lunch wraps" — now you've got my attention. Show me you get it, and I'll trust you.

### Key Insights

- **The leftover-to-lunch bridge** is a powerful concept. The app should explicitly connect dinner leftovers to next-day lunch suggestions, framing it as a transformation rather than just reheating.
- **Kid-friendly filtering** goes beyond simple tags. The app needs to understand texture preferences (no "mixed" foods) and visibility preferences (no visible onion) as distinct from allergies. A per-person preference profile within a household is essential.
- **Household sharing** is a practical need: a shared meal plan visible to all adults in the household could reduce daily coordination friction and allow real-time adjustments (guest counts, last-minute pickups).

## Interview 3: "The Budget-Conscious Student"

### Participant Profile

| Field | Details |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Priya Sharma |
| **Age range** | 23-25 |
| **Household size** | 1 (has 2 roommates, but they don't share groceries) |
| **Cooking comfort level** | Beginner — can make rice, pasta, basic dal, but intimidated by most recipes |
| **Typical weekly grocery spend** | $40-55 CAD |

### Context

Priya is an international graduate student at the University of Alberta. Her food budget is extremely tight — she stretches every dollar and shops at No Frills and the international grocery store on 97th Street. She shares a kitchen with two roommates, which means limited fridge space and sometimes limited stove access. She often looks in her fridge, sees "nothing," and orders $15 of Skip the Dishes — even though she has rice, lentils, frozen peas, and eggs that could easily make a meal. She wastes food not because she buys too much, but because she doesn't know what to make with what she has.

### Interview Transcript

**Interviewer:** Priya, tell me about a time recently when you ended up ordering food instead of cooking.

**Priya:** Last Tuesday. I came home from the lab around 8 PM, starving. I opened the fridge and I saw... some leftover rice from two days ago, a sad-looking zucchini, half a block of paneer, and some yogurt. I also had onions and canned chickpeas in the cupboard. But all I could think was "there's nothing to eat." Like, my brain couldn't see a meal in that. So I ordered butter chicken from Skip. Fifteen dollars. And the whole time I was eating it, I was thinking about how that fifteen dollars could have bought groceries for two days.

**Interviewer:** Looking back at that list of ingredients — rice, zucchini, paneer, yogurt, onions, chickpeas — what could you have made?

**Priya:** *laughs* I mean, when you list it like that, I could have made fried rice with zucchini and paneer. Or chickpea curry with rice. Or even just paneer with yogurt sauce and rice. But in the moment, when I'm tired and hungry, I don't think that way. I see individual ingredients, not meals. It's like having letters but not being able to see the words.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned your budget is tight. How does food waste affect you?

**Priya:** It physically hurts. Like, when I throw away that zucchini because it went mushy, that's a dollar fifty I just put in the garbage. My mom would be horrified. Back home, we wasted almost nothing — my mom could make a meal out of literally anything. I didn't inherit that skill. I feel like I need someone to just tell me: "you have these seven things, here are three meals you can make, and you don't need to buy anything else." That would change my life.

**Interviewer:** How do you currently track what's in your fridge and pantry?

**Priya:** I don't. I just open the fridge and look. Sometimes I forget what's in the back. Last month I found a container of cream cheese behind the milk that had been there for... I don't want to say how long. If there was a way to just take a photo of my grocery receipt and have the app know what I bought, that would be so much easier than typing everything in. I'm not going to sit there and manually enter "one zucchini, 400g paneer, 750ml yogurt." I just won't do it.

**Interviewer:** What kind of recipes would actually work for your situation?

**Priya:** Simple. Like, five ingredients or less. One pot if possible, because my roommate Aisha is usually using one of the burners for her tea, and Derek is sometimes making dinner at the same time. And I need it to tell me how long it takes — not recipe-blog "30 minutes" which actually means 55 minutes once you include all the prep they don't count. Real time. And if it could tell me "this costs approximately $3.50 to make" versus the $15 delivery order, that would motivate me so much.

**Interviewer:** When you do cook successfully, how does that feel?

**Priya:** Amazing, honestly. Last weekend I made aloo gobi from a YouTube video and it actually turned out really good. I was so proud. I even texted my mom a photo. It's not that I can't cook — it's that I don't trust myself to look at random ingredients and make something edible. If an app could give me that confidence on a Tuesday night, not just a relaxed Sunday, that would be the difference.

**Interviewer:** What would make you keep using this app after the first week?

**Priya:** If it stopped me from ordering delivery even twice in a week, it would pay for itself. But honestly, the moment it shows me a meal I can make from what I already have — no grocery trip required — and I actually make it and it tastes good? I'm hooked. That's the magic moment. "You have everything you need" is the most powerful sentence this app could show me.

### Key Insights

- **The "no grocery trip required" feasibility check** is the killer feature for budget-conscious users. The app's primary value proposition for this segment is showing that meals are possible with existing inventory, not suggesting things that require a store run.
- **Receipt scanning for input** is likely the minimum viable data entry method. Manual ingredient entry is a non-starter for many users. A photo of a grocery receipt dramatically lowers the friction of getting inventory into the app.
- **Confidence and emotional reinforcement** matter as much as the recipe itself. The app's tone should encourage and validate, especially for beginner cooks who feel they "can't" cook with random ingredients.

## Interview 4: "The Health-Focused Couple"

### Participant Profile

| Field | Details |
|---|---|
| **Name** | David Chen & Lin Nakamura |
| **Age range** | Late 30s |
| **Household size** | 2 |
| **Cooking comfort level** | Skilled — both enjoy cooking and regularly try new cuisines |
| **Typical weekly grocery spend** | $160-200 CAD |

### Context

David and Lin are a dual-income couple with no kids. They're both health-conscious and enjoy cooking together on weekends. David is lactose-intolerant (not allergic — can handle small amounts of aged cheese, but no milk, cream, or soft cheese). Lin avoids red meat for environmental reasons but eats poultry and fish. They're great at dinner — they plan elaborate meals and cook together three or four nights a week. But breakfast has become a joyless routine (the same green smoothie every morning for the past eight months), and they consistently waste herbs and specialty ingredients because they buy them for one recipe and then don't use the rest before they go bad.

### Interview Transcript

**Interviewer:** You two sound like confident cooks. Where do you feel the system breaks down?

**David:** Breakfast. It's embarrassing, actually. We can make a perfectly good Thai red curry from scratch on a Wednesday, but every single morning it's the same smoothie. Spinach, banana, protein powder, almond milk. Every. Single. Morning. For eight months. We're bored out of our minds but we can't seem to break the habit because it's fast and we don't have to think about it.

**Lin:** And the herbs. Oh my god, the herbs. We'll buy a bunch of cilantro for one recipe, use maybe a third of it, and the rest turns to slime in the fridge within four days. Same with fresh basil, dill, lemongrass, green onions. We spend maybe fifteen dollars a week on herbs and specialty ingredients and waste half of it.

**Interviewer:** Have you tried planning meals around using up those herbs?

**Lin:** We've talked about it, but it never happens naturally. Like, we'll make a Vietnamese pho on Monday that uses cilantro and Thai basil, and then Tuesday's recipe is an Italian pasta that doesn't use either. By Thursday when we make something that needs cilantro again, it's gone. The planning never accounts for "use the rest of the cilantro within three days."

**Interviewer:** David, how does the lactose intolerance affect meal planning?

**David:** It's manageable now because we've adapted, but it was a big adjustment. The tricky part isn't the obvious stuff — I know I can't have a glass of milk. It's the hidden dairy. So many recipes use butter, cream, or soft cheese as a base. The app would need to know that I can have aged parmesan in small amounts — that's fine — but not ricotta or beurre blanc. It's a spectrum, not a binary.

**Interviewer:** What about the breakfast problem specifically — what would a better morning look like?

**David:** Variety that doesn't cost us time. We're out the door by 7:30. So we need something that takes 10 minutes or less, or that we prepped the night before. The smoothie works because it's five minutes. But I'd love it if the app said "you have eggs, that leftover roasted sweet potato from dinner, and feta — make a quick frittata, 12 minutes" or "prep this chia pudding tonight, it's ready in the morning."

**Lin:** And it should use what we already have. If we bought a big thing of Greek yogurt — well, David can't have that, but I can — the app should suggest yogurt parfait for my breakfast and something dairy-free for David's. Not the same breakfast for both of us, because our restrictions are different.

**Interviewer:** How do you think about ingredients that span multiple meals?

**Lin:** That's the dream, honestly. If the app could say "buy one bunch of cilantro, and here's how it gets used across three meals this week — pho Monday, black bean bowls Wednesday, and a cilantro-lime rice with fish Friday" — that would solve the herb waste problem overnight. The cross-meal coordination is what we can't do in our heads. Each meal feels like an island.

**Interviewer:** What would an ideal week of breakfasts look like with this app?

**David:** Different things at least three mornings. Maybe smoothie Monday and Thursday because it's easy, but overnight oats on Tuesday, that sweet potato frittata on Wednesday, avocado toast with a poached egg on Friday. And each one should use something that's already in the fridge — not require a special trip for, like, chia seeds we'll use once and forget about.

**Lin:** Yes. And the app should know that if it suggests overnight oats for both of us, David's version uses almond milk and mine can use regular yogurt. Same concept, different executions. That's what "understanding your household" actually means.

### Key Insights

- **Cross-meal ingredient coordination** is a sophisticated planning feature that even skilled cooks struggle with. The app should plan herb and specialty ingredient usage across the full 3-day plan, not recipe by recipe, to minimize waste.
- **Dietary profiles need nuance beyond binary restrictions.** David's lactose intolerance is a spectrum (aged cheese is fine, soft cheese is not). The app should allow graduated dietary preferences, not just on/off toggles.
- **Breakfast variety is a universal pain point,** even for skilled cooks. The app's breakfast suggestions should emphasize variety, speed, and connection to existing inventory — and should support different dietary profiles within the same household for the same meal.

## Interview 5: "The Reluctant Planner"

### Participant Profile

| Field | Details |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Tom Bergeron |
| **Age range** | 53-57 |
| **Household size** | 1 (recently divorced, lives alone for the first time in 25 years) |
| **Cooking comfort level** | Beginner — can grill meat and make sandwiches, limited beyond that |
| **Typical weekly grocery spend** | $80-120 CAD (erratic — sometimes $30, sometimes $150) |

### Context

Tom is a construction project manager who, until 18 months ago, had a wife who handled all the meal planning and most of the cooking. Since the divorce, he's been struggling to feed himself. He buys ingredients for recipes he finds on Google, but the recipes feel overwhelming once he's actually standing in the kitchen. He has a freezer full of meat he bought on sale, a fridge with condiments and whatever he bought on his last impulse grocery trip, and a pantry with random cans and boxes. He eats out 4-5 times a week and knows it's unsustainable financially and health-wise.

### Interview Transcript

**Interviewer:** Tom, how did you used to think about food before you were cooking for yourself?

**Tom:** I didn't. That's the honest answer. Karen handled all of it. Groceries, planning, cooking — I mean, I'd grill steaks in the summer and make breakfast on the weekends, but the day-to-day? That was her. I didn't appreciate how much work it was until I had to do it myself. Now I'm standing in the grocery store with no list, buying random stuff, and then staring at it at home wondering what I was thinking.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through a recent failed attempt to cook something.

**Tom:** Okay. Two weeks ago I found a recipe for chicken parmesan online. Looked great. Went to the store, bought chicken breasts, breadcrumbs, marinara sauce, mozzarella. Got home, realized I needed eggs for the breading and didn't have any. Went back to the store. Then I read the recipe more carefully and it said "pound the chicken to even thickness." I don't own a meat mallet. I tried using a heavy mug. It didn't go well. The whole thing took me an hour and a half and it was... edible. Barely. I ate half and put the rest in the fridge where it sat for five days before I threw it out. That experience kind of killed my motivation for a while.

**Interviewer:** When you open your fridge now, what do you see?

**Tom:** Ketchup, mustard, mayo, a bag of pre-shredded cheese, some deli turkey that's probably past its date, half an onion in cling wrap, a couple of beers, and I think there's some leftover Chinese food from the weekend. Oh, and butter. I always have butter. The freezer is where the real inventory is — I've got probably twenty pounds of various frozen meat in there from Costco. Chicken breasts, ground beef, pork chops. But I never think to thaw anything in advance, so it's all useless at 6 PM when I'm hungry.

**Interviewer:** If an app was going to help you, what would the first experience need to feel like?

**Tom:** Not overwhelming. I've downloaded three cooking apps and deleted all of them within a week. They all wanted me to set up profiles and enter my dietary preferences and connect my grocery store loyalty card and whatever. I just want to point my phone at my fridge and have it tell me what to make. Or take a picture of my Costco receipt. Keep it simple. Don't make me feel stupid for not knowing what "julienne" means.

**Interviewer:** That's really helpful. How do you feel about the actual act of cooking?

**Tom:** It's complicated. Part of me wants to learn — my daughter said she'd be impressed if I could make her a real dinner when she visits. That means something to me. But every time I try, there's some step I don't understand or some tool I don't have, and I feel like an idiot. I'm a guy who manages million-dollar construction projects, and I can't make rice without burning it. It's humbling. I think... I need the app to be patient with me. Not condescending. Just clear. "Step one: do this. Step two: do this." No assumptions that I know what blanching means.

**Interviewer:** What about the emotional side — is there something beyond just the practical problem of feeding yourself?

**Tom:** *pauses* Yeah. Cooking for one is lonely. When Karen was here, the kitchen was busy. There was a routine. Now it's just me, heating up a frozen pizza at 8 PM, watching TV. If this app gave me a plan — like, "here's what you're making this week, Tom" — I think that structure would actually help. It's not just about food. It's about having a sense that I've got my life together. That I'm taking care of myself. That's a weird thing to ask from an app, but there it is.

**Interviewer:** If you could take a photo of your grocery receipt and the app figured out what you bought — would you actually use it?

**Tom:** That's the only way I'd use it. I'm not typing in "500 grams boneless skinless chicken breast" into a text field. I'll take a photo. I can do that. And if the app then told me "you have enough for three dinners this week, here they are, and here's what to thaw tonight for tomorrow" — I mean, that's the dream. That's what Karen used to do. Not to be weird about it, but the app would basically be doing what she did, and I'd be grateful for it.

### Key Insights

- **Onboarding must be nearly frictionless.** Tom has already deleted three cooking apps. The first interaction needs to deliver value immediately — not ask for extensive profile setup. A receipt photo or fridge photo as the first action creates an instant "wow" moment.
- **Recipe instructions need to assume nothing.** Beginner users need step-by-step language that avoids jargon, specifies exact tools, and includes time estimates for each step — not just the total cook time.
- **The emotional dimension of meal planning is real.** For some users, the app's tone and structure provide a sense of competence and self-care that goes beyond nutrition. The app should feel encouraging without being patronizing — more "coach" than "teacher."

## Synthesis: Common Themes Across Interviews

| Theme | Scenarios Where It Appeared | Implication for What the Fridge |
|---|---|---|
| **Use-first prioritization** (items nearing expiration should drive suggestions) | All 5 (Anika's wilting produce, Marcus's broccoli, Priya's zucchini, David & Lin's herbs, Tom's deli turkey) | The meal plan algorithm should weight ingredient shelf life heavily, surfacing "use tonight" items at the top of every suggestion. |
| **Breakfast variety and speed** | 1 (Anika), 2 (Marcus), 4 (David & Lin) | Breakfast is the most neglected meal across user types. Suggestions should emphasize sub-10-minute prep, night-before options, and variety even when inventory is limited. |
| **Dietary restrictions handling** | 2 (Marcus — kids' texture/visibility preferences), 4 (David & Lin — lactose spectrum, red meat avoidance) | The app needs a flexible dietary profile system that goes beyond simple allergies: texture aversions, visibility preferences, spectrum tolerances, and ethical choices should all be configurable per household member. |
| **Budget consciousness and waste reduction** | 1 (Anika), 3 (Priya), 5 (Tom) | Cost-per-meal estimates, "no grocery trip needed" indicators, and weekly waste-reduction metrics would resonate strongly with multiple user segments. |
| **Onboarding and low-friction input** | 3 (Priya — receipt scanning), 5 (Tom — deleted three apps) | Receipt photo capture should be a Day 1 feature, not a roadmap item. Manual ingredient entry will cause abandonment in the users who need the app most. |
| **Cross-meal ingredient coordination** | 2 (Marcus — leftover-to-lunch bridge), 4 (David & Lin — herbs across meals) | The 3-day meal plan should be optimized holistically, not meal by meal. Ingredient usage should span breakfast, lunch, and dinner to minimize waste and maximize variety. |
| **Emotional confidence in cooking** | 3 (Priya — "I can't see the words"), 5 (Tom — feeling humbled, wanting structure) | The app's UX copy, tone, and suggestion framing should build confidence. | **Leftover reuse and transformation** | 1 (Anika — single-serving challenge), 2 (Marcus — dinner-to-lunch bridge) | The app should explicitly suggest leftover transformations: not "reheat Tuesday's pasta" but "use Tuesday's roast chicken in Wednesday's lunch wraps." Transformation reframes leftovers as intentional, not wasteful. |

## Recommendations

**Prioritize "use what you have" over "discover new recipes."** Across all five scenarios, the most consistent user need is not recipe discovery — it is the ability to see *meals* where they currently see only disconnected ingredients. The app's core value loop should be: user inputs inventory (ideally via receipt scan), app shows feasible meals ranked by ingredient urgency, user cooks with confidence. Every design decision should be evaluated against this loop. If a feature (social sharing, gamification, grocery delivery integration) does not directly strengthen this core loop, it should be deferred.

**Design for the whole day, not just dinner.** Four of five interviews revealed that breakfast and lunch are where planning breaks down — even for users who handle dinner well. Marcus's lunchbox chaos, Anika's toast autopilot, David and Lin's smoothie rut, and Tom's skipped meals all point to the same insight: dinner gets disproportionate attention in meal planning tools, but the biggest quality-of-life improvements may come from solving breakfast and lunch. The 3-day plan should treat all three meals as first-class citizens, with explicit connections between them (leftover dinner chicken becomes tomorrow's lunch wrap; tonight's 2-minute prep becomes tomorrow's grab-and-go breakfast).

**Build for a spectrum of cooking skill, not a single persona.** The gap between Priya (beginner, five-ingredient limit) and David and Lin (skilled, making Thai curry from scratch) is enormous, and the app needs to serve both. This suggests a recipe complexity layer: every meal suggestion should indicate difficulty level, active prep time, total time, and number of required tools. Advanced users can filter for ambitious meals; beginners should see only what they can realistically execute. Tom's experience — overwhelmed by a recipe that assumed he owned a meat mallet — is a cautionary tale. If the app's default suggestions are too complex for its least confident users, those users will churn immediately.

**Treat data entry as a design problem, not a user obligation.** Priya and Tom both made clear that manual ingredient entry is a dealbreaker. Receipt scanning, and eventually photo-based fridge inventory, should be core input methods from launch. The friction of getting inventory data into the app is the single biggest barrier to adoption. Every unnecessary manual step is a user who opens the app once, sighs, and orders delivery instead. The app's intelligence is only as good as the data it has, and users will only provide that data if the input method respects their time and energy.
